# YM2612 notes

The hardware record for `src/mdd/chip/Ym2612.hx`, `Channel.hx` and `Operator.hx`: what the part does
that no general rule predicts, and how it was established.

Everything below was established by measurement against Nuked OPN2, built as a program of its own so
that its licence never reaches this repository. A claim here means a fixture went from disagreeing to
bit identical when the behaviour was implemented.

`mdd gate chip` is that measurement, and it runs here: `mdd.gate.Fixtures` writes 1032 register
scripts, `tools/opn2/opn2.c` renders each one through the reference, and the cores render the same
scripts and are compared sample for sample. All 1032 are bit identical.

Two other sources stand behind it: the Sega Genesis Technical Manual's YM2612 section, which is the
part's own documentation and is quoted below where it agrees and where it does not, and the
reference's own source, which is a reverse engineering of the die. Reading the second settled in
minutes several things that fitting curves to its output did not, and where a rule below says it was
read rather than measured, that is what it means.

## The domains

**The envelope counts attenuation, not amplitude.** Total level, the envelope and the sine are all
added in that domain and one exponential turns the total into a number. 256 units of attenuation is
6 dB, so the envelope's own unit is 0.09375 dB and its full 1023 is the part's documented 96 dB.

**Total level joins the envelope before the pair saturate.** The sum of the envelope and total level
saturates at 1023 and is only then shifted left by two into the attenuation domain. Adding them after
the shift, which is the obvious reading, lets a quiet operator go further into silence than the part
allows. Measured: a total level of 16 divides the amplitude by four, 32 by sixteen and 64 by 256, so
one unit of total level is 0.75 dB and eight units of envelope.

**The operator output is fourteen bits signed.** The exponential's mantissa carries an implied
leading one and is shifted left by two before the exponent shifts it back down. An earlier version
was four times too quiet, which left every algorithm that modulates unrecognisable while the four
parallel operators of algorithm seven sounded almost right.

**A channel takes each operator as nine bits, one at a time.** Every operator that reaches the output
is added to the channel accumulator as `output >> 5`, and the accumulator saturates to
`[-256, 255]` **on each addition**, not once at the end. Summing in fourteen bits and narrowing once
gives a different answer wherever a channel is loud.

## The tables

**The sine and exponential tables are the documented formulas and need no data.** `logsin[i]` is
`round(-log2(sin((i + 0.5) * pi / 512)) * 256)` and `exp[i]` is `round((2^(i/256) - 1) * 1024)`.
Both reproduce the reference's tables entry for entry, so nothing is copied.

**Detune is computed, not looked up.** The widely published 32 entry per amount table is wrong at key
code 4 with detune 3, where it gives 3 and the part gives 2. The part builds it from eight values,
`[16, 17, 19, 20, 22, 24, 27, 29]`, a sum of the key code's block and a constant per amount, and a
shift. `Channel.detuneOf` is that computation. Every other one of the 128 entries agrees.

**The envelope's step is two tables, not one.** Below rate 48 the rate says how rarely the envelope
moves, as a power of two, and one of four eight step patterns says which of those steps count. From
48 up it moves on every step and the step itself doubles instead, through a four by four table
indexed by the low two bits of the rate and of the counter. Both were read back out of the reference
a rate at a time, with the counter recorded, so their phase is pinned and not merely their shape.

**Sustain level 15 is matched against 62.** The four bit field becomes 31 for 15 and stays itself
otherwise, and decay gives way to sustain when the envelope's top six bits **equal** that doubled,
not when they pass it. Treating 15 as 31 rather than 62 puts the knee an octave of attenuation too
early.

**An envelope is spent before it is full.** Once the top six bits are all ones, which is 1008 and
above, the part stops stepping, snaps the envelope to 1023 and moves to release. Waiting for 1023
exactly leaves a tail a few units long that the part does not have.

## The rotation

A sample is twenty four internal cycles and each one belongs to one operator of one channel. The
channel is the cycle modulo six and the turn is the cycle divided by six.

**The operator order is S1, S3, S2, S4**, the same interleave the register map uses.

**Three modulation paths arrive a whole sample late**, because the operator they feed has its turn
before the operator that feeds it: S1 into S3, S2 into S3, and S2 into S4. The other three, S1 into
S2, S1 into S4 and S3 into S4, arrive within the sample. Getting this wrong leaves the four parallel
algorithms exact and every chained one wrong, which is what it looked like before it was found.

**The envelope reaches the output a turn after it moves.** An operator speaks with the envelope as it
stood before this sample's step. Stepping first makes every note with a moving envelope one step too
far along.

**The envelope generator runs every sample.** Only the size of the step is held back to one sample in
three. Every transition, attack reaching zero, decay reaching the sustain level, a key going up, an
envelope becoming spent, is decided every single sample. A key lifted between two steps is therefore
heard on the sample it was lifted on.

**An attack too fast to hear lands on the key itself.** Where the scaled attack rate is 62 or more the
envelope goes to zero at the key down rather than at the next step.

## Keys and writes

**The key register's four bits run in operator order**, S1 to S4, and not the slot order that the
rest of the register map uses. This is the one place the interleave does not apply. Established by
keying one operator at a time with four different multiples and hearing which pitch came out.

**A channel takes all four key states at once, on its own first turn of the rotation.** That turn
belongs to operator one, which has therefore already had its turn when the change lands, so operator
one always starts and stops one sample after the other three. This is not a property of when the
write happened: it holds for every channel and every write time.

**Register writes are applied lazily**, when the rotation reaches the slot or channel the address
names, so the delay depends on which channel and which operator is being written and can be anything
up to a full rotation. A slot register lands where `cycle % 12` is `(group low bit) * 6 + channel`,
and a channel register where `cycle % 6` is the channel; everything shared by the whole chip is taken
as soon as it is seen. `Ym2612.lands` is that rule, and `WRITE_LATENCY` is only how long the write
takes to become something the part will act on at all.

Implementing it changed no fixture on its own, since every fixture here writes its registers long
before it presses a key. What it did change is that the tolerance on `WRITE_LATENCY` collapsed: with
a flat delay a range of values gave the same samples, and with the rotation deciding only one does.

**The phase does not advance on the turn it begins again.** A turn that resets an operator's phase is
spent on the reset: the part clears the increment first and then adds it, so nothing moves. Resetting
and then stepping in the same turn looks harmless, and it is, right up until it is used to cancel out
a key press landing a sample late. Both faults together sounded exactly right on anything without
feedback and a moving envelope, and were what every remaining group was failing on.

**An operator speaks with the phase it had, and only then begins it again.** The turn a phase restarts
on is the last turn of the old one rather than the first of the new. Zeroing the phase before the
operator speaks is silent either way on a key press from nothing, since the envelope is shut, and
audible the moment something already sounding is keyed again. CSM is where that shows.

**Beginning the envelope again and beginning the phase again are different questions.** A key press
asks for both. The SSG shapes ask for one or the other: two of the eight restart the phase, six
restart the envelope, and only the two that name the reset touch the phase. Restarting the phase
whenever the envelope restarts leaves the two alternating shapes drifting by a fraction of a cycle,
which reads as a small amplitude error and is not one.

## The converter

**The converter visits channels in the order 1, 5, 3, 0, 4, 2**, one every four cycles, and holds
each for three of them. Three of the six are reached before they have handed anything on within that
sample, so **channels 1, 3 and 5 are heard one sample behind channels 0, 2 and 4**. Without this,
five of the six channels are individually exact and no two of them are exact together, which is what
made a six channel patch fail where each of its channels passed alone.

## The SSG envelope

Four bits per operator, register 0x90, and the top one turns the other three on. They invert what the
envelope is doing, turn it round each time it reaches the halfway mark, and either stop it there or
start it again.

- The envelope is spent at 512 rather than at 1008, and every step outside attack is four times as
  large.
- A key going up hands back whatever the shape was showing, as the envelope itself, so an inverted
  envelope releases from where it looked like it was rather than from where it was.
- A restart while the envelope is already attacking keeps the attack moving rather than beginning it
  again from nothing, and is timed by the attack rate whatever the envelope was doing before.
- Two of the shapes hold at the top instead of starting again, and holding also stops the envelope
  being called spent.

## Reading a chip that will not say what it is doing

Every one of the last four faults was found the same way and none of them was found by reasoning. The
reference is a program, so its internals can be printed: the phase, the operator output, the channel
accumulator and what it handed on, for one slot, every sample. Printing the same four from here and
lining them up says which of them first disagrees and on which sample, and that names the operation.

Two chips compared only at their outputs cannot tell a late key press from a wrong envelope from a
wrong phase, because each of them can hide the others. Comparing at the outputs is what a suite is
for; comparing inside is what fixing one is for.

`OpnCheck` keeps both: `--envelope` prints what the four envelopes of channel one held around the
sample the two parted, and `--inside` prints the phase, the operator output, the accumulator and what
left the chip.

## The oscillator

**The divider compares bits, not magnitudes.** The counter deciding when the oscillator's phase moves
is matched against one of `[108, 77, 71, 67, 62, 44, 8, 5]` by rate, and it steps when every bit of
that value is set in it. Counted up from nothing that is the same as counting to it, so these read as
a number of samples; the difference only shows when the rate changes while the divider is already
part way up, and it is what the part does.

**The divider runs from reset whether or not the oscillator is enabled.** Only the phase counter is
held. Enabling the oscillator therefore does not start its cycle from the beginning, and where in the
cycle it starts depends on how long the machine had been running.

**Turning the oscillator off does not turn the tremolo off.** The phase counter is held at zero, and
zero is the counter's deepest tremolo, so a channel asking for tremolo with the oscillator stopped is
attenuated by the full depth it asked for and stays there. `lfo-tremolo-stopped` covers it.

**The tremolo and the vibrato reach the operators a sample after the counter moves.** The counter
steps at the first cycle of a sample and what it is worth arrives at the first cycle of the next.

**The tremolo is a triangle of 126 down to 0 and back**, `126 - 2n` over the counter's first 64 steps
and `2(n - 64)` over the rest, shifted right by 7, 3, 1 or 0 by the depth in 0xB4 before it joins the
envelope. Those four shifts are the documented 0, 1.4, 5.9 and 11.8 dB.

**The vibrato is two shifted copies of the note's top seven bits, added and shifted again.** All 64
combinations of depth and step were read out of the reference across all 128 values of those seven
bits and fitted; `Channel.VIBRATO` is the result, packed three shifts to an entry. The sum joins the
note at twice its own width and carries no further than twelve bits, so a high note under the deepest
vibrato wraps rather than saturating.

**The manual's oscillator frequencies do not match.** It gives 3.98, 5.56, 6.02, 6.37, 6.88, 9.63,
48.1 and 72.2 Hz, and every one of those is exactly `clock / (144 * 128 * n)` at 8 MHz for `n` one
larger than the values above. The reference steps on the values above, measured directly. One of the
two is wrong by a part in a hundred and nothing here can say which, so what is implemented is what
the reference does and this is written down.

## The frequency high byte is a register, not a latch shared by the part

**0xA4 to 0xA6 are three registers on each half, one per channel, and 0xAC to 0xAE are three
more for channel three's separate operators.** Writing the high byte holds it until the low
byte at 0xA0 to 0xA2 arrives, and the pair that lands is the pair belonging to that channel.

A driver is free to write all six high bytes and then all six low bytes, and hardware answers
correctly because each channel kept its own. A core holding one high byte for the whole part
gives every channel in such a run the last one written, which reads as most of a chord landing
a block or two away from where it was asked for. Nothing warns, and a driver that writes each
pair adjacently, which is the common case and what this application produces, never shows it.

## Channel three

**Each of its four operators can play a note of its own**, through registers 0xA8 to 0xAE, and any of
the three nonzero settings of 0x27's top two bits turns that on. The manual calls two of those three
illegal.

**Which register drives which operator was measured, and the manual disagrees.** Writing one at a
time and hearing which pitch moved gives 0xA9 to the first operator, 0xAA to the second, 0xA8 to the
third, and the channel's own 0xA0 and 0xA4 to the fourth. The manual assigns 0xA8, 0xA9 and 0xAA to
operators two, three and four in that order, and the channel's own to operator one. The suite is bit
identical on the measured assignment across 66 fixtures.

**Each operator's key code follows the note it is playing**, so its rate scaling, its detune and the
scaling of its vibrato all move with it rather than with the channel's.

**The mode belongs to channel three alone.** The same registers do nothing to any other channel,
which `special-elsewhere-*` covers.

## CSM

The setting the manual calls illegal, `0b10` in the top bits of 0x27, is CSM.

**Timer A running out presses every key of channel three for one sample.** Starting the timer is
itself such a press, on the same rising edge that loads it.

**That key reaches all four operators at once**, unlike the key register, which reaches operator one a
sample after the other three. The CSM key does not pass through the key register at all, which is why
it does not inherit its timing.

**A CSM key folds each operator's own total level into its envelope**, as a bitwise or, before that
sample's envelope step is added. A channel already sounding is therefore pushed back towards silence
every time the timer runs out. This was read in the reference's source after being mistaken for a
magic constant: on the fixture that found it the total level was 10, and ten shifted left three times
is 0x50.

**Total level does not reach the output at all in CSM mode.** Channel three plays as though every
operator were at total level zero. Measured across the whole range, and confirmed in the source.

## The discrete converter

**The discrete part and the YM3438 disagree only here.** The YM3438 drives a channel's value for
three of the four cycles it holds and rests at zero for the fourth. The discrete part drives it for
one and rests at a step whose sign is the value's for the other three, and it adds one to any value
that is not negative. Each channel therefore contributes `value + 1 + 3` where it is positive and
`value - 3` where it is negative, and a channel panned away from a side still leaves four times its
step on that side. That is the ladder effect, and it is why a Model 1 crosses zero through a gap.

`Ym2612.discrete` chooses between them and is the discrete part by default, since that is the part
this machine carries. The reference has both modes and the comparison program takes a `ladder` argument
for the second, so the `discrete-*` fixtures are rendered on one part and everything else on the
other. Forcing the ladder on for the whole suite takes it from 1032 of 1032 to 61, which is how it
was confirmed that the fixtures tell the two apart.

The reference marks its own ladder emulation as not verified against hardware. This one reproduces it
exactly and inherits that caveat.

## Writes and status

**A data write leaves the part busy for 32 of its internal cycles.** An address write does not.
Reading any of the four addresses answers the two timer flags and that busy bit, and SGDK spins on it
before every write it makes.

## A key on does not always name a semitone

`mdd gate vgm` reads the F number at every key on and asks how far it sits from the nearest
semitone. Across the corpus on 5 September 2026, by source:

| source | key ons within a quarter tone |
| --- | --- |
| Sonic the Hedgehog | 99.5 per cent |
| Sonic the Hedgehog 2 | 99.8 per cent |
| Sonic the Hedgehog 3 | 98.8 per cent |
| Mickey Mania | 20.7 per cent |

The three Sonic soundtracks agree with the importer to within rounding, which is what says the F
number reading is right. **Mickey Mania does not, and that is unexplained.** A driver that slides
into a note reads low here, because the note is read where the slide starts rather than where it
arrives, and that accounts for the odd few per cent elsewhere. It does not obviously account for four
key ons in five.

Two things worth ruling out before anything is concluded: that the driver carries its own F number
table pitched away from equal temperament, and that the importer is reading its key ons at the wrong
moment. Neither has been checked. The figure is printed per source every run so that a change in it
is visible, and the check asks only that the best source stays above 99, which is what would catch a
regression in the reading itself.

## A release a driver never lets be heard

Mickey Mania's driver leaves the release rate at nought on nearly every carrier, at the key on and
at the key off alike. Read from the 24 files in the corpus on 14 September 2026:

| carriers' release rates at a key off | key offs |
| --- | --- |
| 0, 0 | 7451 |
| 0, 0, 0 | 4769 |
| 0 | 3752 |
| 9, 1 | 595 |

The driver does not end a note with its release. The next key on on the same channel follows a key
off by a median of 9 samples, and a rest is a total level of 127 written after the key off, 1184
times within half a second of one. A release of nought is never heard in the game, and a patch
captured at a key on keeps it, so the same patch keyed off anywhere else sounds on for as long as
the slowest release takes.

The Mickey Mania bank therefore ships every operator whose release rate is 2 or below at 15, the
fastest, which is what the game's own key off amounts to: 106 operators across all 27 of its
patches. The Sonic banks have two patches between them with a carrier that slow, and keep them as
captured.


## How many writes a frame holds

Measured across the 112 files in `vendor/vgm`, 281,354 frames at 60 Hz, counting every PSG write and
every YM2612 data write except converter data, which the Z80 streams on its own timer rather than
the driver writing in its frame loop:

| | writes in one frame |
| --- | --- |
| the busiest frame of all | 1016, in IceCap Zone Act 1 |
| the busiest once a file is playing | 178, in Sonic 3's unreleased Staff Roll |
| 99.9 per cent of frames | 91 or fewer |

The 1016 is an opening burst: a driver setting every register before anything sounds, which it can
afford because nothing is playing yet. IceCap's lands more than a second in, after a long intro,
which is why it shows up in the second row of that table as well as the first. Past the first second
nothing else in the corpus exceeds 178.

`Profile.perFrame` is 178 for the Mega Drive, and `Budget.overStream` warns above it, skipping the
first frame for the same reason the measurement does. **The number is a floor on what the hardware
does, not a proof of its limit**: it says 178 is known to work because a shipped game does it, and
says nothing about 179. It is here to catch automation asking for far more than any game ever did,
not to draw an exact line.

Re-measure with `mdd gate check`, which prints the three figures every run.

**What the driver mode does with those numbers.** `mdd.play.Driver` holds each frame to `perFrame`
writes and puts the rest into the next frame, keeping an address and its value together because
splitting them lands the value against a different register. It leaves the opening frame alone for
the same reason the measurement above does. Converter data is not counted and not delayed: the
second processor streams it on its own timer, which is the other half of the frame stall in
`Song.stallAt`.

The busy period turns out not to bind. Thirty two internal cycles is about 25 microseconds, so the
part will take some forty thousand writes a second, around 666 a frame, where a driver's own time
runs out at 178. **On this machine the code writing the registers is the limit, not the chip taking
them.** The gap is still modelled, and it is what separates two writes that would otherwise land on
the same tick.

Measured with the mode on over thirty seconds of Green Hill: 448,144 register writes either way,
none late and none lost, which is what says the filter is inert on music a real driver already
produced. Against a ceiling of 24, a quarter of what a busy song asks for, no frame past the first
exceeds it, the order is kept, and what could not be delivered is counted rather than passed over.

**Where the driver mode should get its timing.** `hx68k` next door carries a 68000 and a Mega Drive
memory map, and its `emulator/src/hx68k/md/Ym2612.hx` holds the bus side this program does not: the
busy period after a data write, counted in the same 32 internal cycles recorded above. That is the
thing to measure a driver model against, by running a driver there and capturing when its writes
actually land, rather than choosing numbers here. It is the same repository rather than somebody
else's emulator, so the provenance rule is satisfied by measuring against it.

## Everything after the chips

The chips are held to the reference sample for sample. Nothing about that says how the machine turns
what they made into something a host can play, and four things there were wrong at once.

**The two chips have to be brought to the same loudness by hand.** The FM chip counts in ninths of a
bit and reaches 1536 either way on each side; the SN76489's four bits of attenuation are two decibels
a step and say nothing about how loud nought is. `Sn76489.LOUDEST` sets one of its channels level with
one FM channel, which is a choice rather than a measurement, and the whole mix then maps onto a
sixteen bit sample so the loudest the part can be is the loudest a device can play.

**The SN76489's output is unipolar.** A channel contributes its amplitude while its output bit is set
and nothing while it is clear, so the whole mix sits off centre by however many of its channels are
sounding. The machine couples its output through a capacitor; `Render.COUPLING` is one pole at about
twenty hertz and is what puts the mix back on zero. It is the only thing in the render path that
could be called a filter, and it is there because the board has one.

**Both chips run far above any host rate.** The FM chip makes 53267 samples a second and the PSG's
squares turn at up to 112 kHz, so reading either at the moment a host sample falls due turns
everything above half the host rate into a tone that is not there. The FM is interpolated between the
two samples either side, which costs one of its own sample periods of delay, and the PSG averages
every one of its own steps between two host samples.

**A running console has a clock of its own and this does not.** In an emulator the machine's clock and
the device's clock are two clocks, one is always the faster, and the drift shows up as a click every
few seconds or a growing delay; the fix there is to bend the sample rate by a few parts in a thousand
against how full the ring is. Nothing here needs that. `Render` is driven by how much room the device
has and by nothing else, so there is one clock and no drift to correct, and no rate bending exists in
this repository.

`mdd gate audio` measures the rest of it: that the render allocates nothing, that it outruns real
time, that the pitch off the rendered waveform is the pitch the F number asks for, and what the ring
adds on top of whatever the driver's own latency is.

## The converter's sample source

There is no `Dac` class and there should not be one. The DAC is a register on the YM2612, 0x2A for
the byte and 0x2B for whether it replaces channel six, and `Ym2612` models it where the hardware puts
it. What deserves a type of its own is the thing that decides which byte to write and when, and that
is a producer of register writes rather than a part: it belongs to `mdd.play.Stream`, with the sample
data in the song.

## Traps in the measuring, not the part

**A WASAPI device asks for more in its first few callbacks than its reported buffer.** The driver
here reports a 480 frame period and a 1056 frame buffer, and priming the ring with 1056 frames before
starting the device still underran three times inside the first 250 ms, every run, and never again
after. Priming 100 ms ends it. The reported buffer is what the device settles to, not what it opens
with, and a producer that primes exactly one buffer is measuring the wrong number.

**The reference harness must put standard output in binary mode on Windows.** Without it every 0x0A
byte of a sample leaves as 0x0D 0x0A, every file is longer than it should be, and the comparison is
measuring a shifted stream. This produced spikes twenty times the amplitude of the signal, once per
waveform period, which read convincingly as a hardware quirk and was not one.

**A patch driven into heavy modulation is chaotic.** Two chips one unit apart diverge completely
within a few hundred samples, so correlation on such a fixture says nothing at all. Only bit identical
agreement is worth reporting, which is what `OpnCheck` reports.

**Total level 0 on a carrier already saturates the channel.** Four operators at full level in
algorithm seven sit pinned at the accumulator's limit, so a fixture written that way measures the
saturation and not the synthesis. Several early fixtures were wrong this way and read as chip faults.

## The key state is read once a slot, not once a write

Register 0x28 arms a channel's four key bits; the channel copies that armed value into its key
request once per slot, which is once per output sample. A key off and a key on that arrive between
two of those copies are one edge, and the envelope never restarts. The chip is right to do this and
so is the emulation, but it means a producer must leave a gap.

Which channels lose the edge follows from when a write lands. A write is held for two slots before
it takes effect, and a write arriving while one is held lands the held one at once, so a key off
followed by a key on on the same sample puts the key off in place immediately and the key on two
slots later. The first channel copies its key request in slot 0, before the key on lands, and sees
the key off; the other five copy theirs after it and never do.

The sequencer writes a key off one host sample early wherever another note keys the same FM channel
on at the sample the first note ends, and leaves every other key off where its note ends. One sample
is enough, because the render applies every write due at an output sample before it steps the chip,
and steps the chip at least once an output sample. Four back to back notes of a patch that decays to
nothing inside a beat, rendered on each FM channel in turn, attack 18 times in 18 with the early key
off and 3 times in 18 with every key off moved onto its key on's sample, all three on the first
channel. Without the gap a legato run plays as one note decaying to its sustain level, while every
register the chip sees agrees with the file.

## A key on with no key off before it is a bend, not a strike

Drivers write 0x28 with the key bits already set to change a note's frequency without re-attacking
it. Reading that as a retrigger inserts an attack the file does not have; reading it as nothing at
all merges two pitches into one note and loses seventy of Green Hill's eight hundred and nine key
ons. The transcription writes a note whose `tied` flag says it continues the one before it, and the
sequencer emits the new frequency without the patch or the key on.

## A key on over a sounding channel clicks

A key on starts every operator's phase again from nought, so a channel still sounding jumps from
wherever its wave stood. `mdd gate declick` renders a 110 Hz sine keyed on again every quarter of a
second through the Mega Drive 2's output stage and measures the energy through a fourth order high
pass at 1.5 kHz, from 1 ms before each key on is heard to 6 ms after, against the note's own level.
The same measure in the middle of the note is the floor: what the tone and the part's own grain put
through the filter.

| key on | as written | declicked | floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| over a held note | -33.7 dB | -39.7 dB | -37.4 dB |
| over a release still sounding | -34.1 dB | -39.7 dB | -37.4 dB |

Declicked, the sequencer writes a release rate of 15 to every operator of the channel and keys it
off 4 ms before the key on, and the next note's patch puts its own release back. Read off the core's
envelope, a release rate of 15 falls 13.2 dB a millisecond: -20 dB at 1.58 ms, -40 dB at 3.1 ms and
-60 dB at 4.56 ms. 14 is barely slower at 11.5 dB a millisecond, and 13 falls half as fast. A lead
of 1.5 ms leaves the note only 20 dB down and still clicks. At 4 ms the key on lands about 50 dB
down, and the fade before it measures at most -37.0 dB, on the floor.

A carrier attacking slower than 26 is left alone. Its key on starts the attack from wherever the
envelope stands, which is what makes a legato swell, and a fade first would be heard as a gap.

The rendered sound runs 212 samples behind the writes at 44100 Hz, the group delay of the 512 tap
resampler, found where a step on the converter is heard half way up. A window placed at the write
rather than at the sound measures the 4.8 ms before the edge instead of the edge.

## A converter hit that stops away from the middle clicks

The converter holds the last byte it is given. A hit cut short by its note, or a recording that ends
away from `$80`, steps there, holds, and steps again when the channel is let go. Measured the same
way on a 150 Hz tone recorded at 16 kHz:

| edge | as written | declicked | floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a hit cut by its note | -34.2 dB | -43.3 dB | -40.4 dB |
| a hit ending away from the middle, where its note ends | -22.8 dB | -44.9 dB | -40.1 dB |

Declicked, the hit returns to the middle along a half cosine over 1.5 ms, at the rate it was
recorded at, before the channel is let go. Where another hit starts exactly where the first was
cut, that hit takes the converter over and nothing is added.

## What the register comparison measures

`mdd gate vgm` replays five games as songs and reads both streams back into a shadow register file,
sampled every 735 samples, comparing only channels the file has keyed. On that measure the
transcription is exact: detune and multiple, key scaling and attack, both decay rates, sustain level
and release, and the SSG envelope are all zero apart; total level is 0.11 to 0.81 apart out of 127
and the two stereo bits 0.26; pitch is 1.8 to 8.2 cents, which is the vibrato a note cannot hold;
and a square sounding in both is 0.04 to 1.85 attenuation steps apart out of fifteen.

Total level and the stereo bits move during a piece, so they are read off an import as automation
rather than baked into the patch, and the held value is written again at every note on. Panning is
two bits and must be stepped, not interpolated: interpolating them turns a channel on the right into
a channel on the left with tremolo.

## What is left on the lead, and what it is not

Green Hill's FM1 renders about a quarter quieter than the file over the
second half, and the cause is not in the register stream. Measured at the
attack of the note at 890820 samples: both write 0x00 then 0xF0 to 0x28
within thirty-six samples of each other, and every register of that channel
agrees, unused bit seven of total level aside, which the core masks off.
Detune and multiple, key scaling and attack, both decay rates, sustain
level and release, SSG, feedback and algorithm are identical, the mean
total level apart on that channel over thirty seconds is 0.56 of 127, the
strike counts are equal and the keyed time is within one part per thousand.

What differs is the shape after the attack: the file holds its peak for a
quarter of a second and then decays, while the transcription decays at once
by about the three decibels its sustain level asks for. Disabling the level
automation changes nothing, so it is not the swept levels, and reading the
patch at the key off rather than the key on changes nothing either, so it
is not a register the driver writes just after keying. The cause is still
open. It is confined to that one channel: the summed output is 0.0464
against the file's 0.0469 and follows its envelope at 0.928.

## The console around the chip

The chip core was right and the sound was still too clean, because what a
listener remembers is not the chip's output but the console's. The chain was:
nine bit per channel clamp, the ladder, a band limited resample to the device
rate, and a DC block at 17.569 Hz for the coupling capacitor. Nothing stood for
the analogue stage between the chip pins and the socket.

The corner was first taken from the documented output stage, at 2840 Hz, and
recorded here as the number most likely to be wrong. It was. Hardware captures
of Sonic the Hedgehog 2 made by the 16-Bit Audiophile Project settle it.

### What the console does

Four tracks were rendered from their own vgm logs with no output stage at all,
and the averaged spectrum of each was divided by the capture of the same track.
The four agree within about a decibel across the whole band, which is what says
this is a system response and not an accident of the music.

| | 1250 Hz | 2000 | 2840 | 4000 | 5000 | 8000 | 12500 | 16000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Emerald Hill | -0.75 | -0.88 | -1.53 | -3.22 | -4.75 | -7.65 | -11.35 | -13.78 |
| Chemical Plant | -0.18 | -0.91 | -1.96 | -3.58 | -5.09 | -8.25 | -12.16 | -14.44 |
| Casino Night | -0.69 | -1.43 | -2.19 | -3.84 | -5.38 | -8.66 | -12.60 | -14.98 |
| Metropolis | -0.31 | -1.27 | -2.19 | -4.00 | -5.46 | -8.44 | -11.60 | -13.95 |
| mean | -0.48 | -1.12 | -1.97 | -3.66 | -5.17 | -8.25 | -11.93 | -14.29 |

A single pole fits that mean to 0.301 dB across 315 Hz to 16 kHz, at **3300 Hz**.
Two poles fit far worse, at 0.89 dB, so the shape is first order and the only
thing wrong with the documented value was the frequency.

### Why the filter is a pole and a zero

The obvious realisation is one line, `y += a * (x - y)`, and it cannot hold that
shape: its response flattens near Nyquist where the console keeps falling. Fitted
on its own terms it reaches only 0.632 dB, and the bilinear transform errs the
other way at 1.004 dB by falling too fast. Both are wrong only above about
10 kHz, but that is where the first attempt left a 2.45 dB error.

Adding a zero fixes it. The pole stays `exp(-2 pi fc / rate)` and the zero is
solved so the response equals the analogue pole at 80 per cent of Nyquist, which
is a quadratic with two reciprocal roots, the one inside the unit circle being
the answer. Nothing is fitted at run time and no constant is tabulated.

| rate | pole | zero | from the analogue pole |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 32000 | 0.5231 | -0.1543 | 0.102 dB |
| 44100 | 0.6249 | -0.1562 | 0.090 dB |
| 48000 | 0.6492 | -0.1566 | 0.090 dB |
| 96000 | 0.8057 | -0.1580 | 0.041 dB |
| 192000 | 0.8976 | -0.1583 | 0.011 dB |

### What it measures at

Rendering the same four tracks again with the stage on leaves a residual against
the captures of between -0.57 and +0.52 dB from 80 Hz to 16 kHz, about 0.31 dB
taken together. The one line version had left 0.685 dB, and the 2.45 dB hole at
the top is gone.

`mdd gate console` renders Emerald Hill and compares it with the capture band by
band. It reads 0.72 dB with the stage on, 2.85 dB with the later console's
stage, and 5.98 dB with none, so it fails on every wrong answer rather than only
on the absent one. It passes when `vendor/console` is empty, saying so.

The captures are recordings of a real console, so the fitted corner absorbs
whatever else stands between this renderer and that recording, the capture chain
included. That is the right thing for the output to sound like the hardware, and
it is worth knowing before quoting 3300 Hz as a property of the console alone.

`MODEL_TWO` at 7100 Hz is still the documented value and has no capture behind
it. The gate check measures it at 2.85 dB against a Model 1 capture, which says
only that it is not that console.

`Mixing` carries the field and `Mixdown` hands it to its own render, so an export
and the speakers pass through the same stage. It is on by default and the Sound
page turns it off.

## Where a carrier stands when a key on arrives

A voice and a loudness are two things on this part, and the split is in the
driver rather than in the chip. The voice table holds the timbre, the driver
holds a volume byte per channel, and the byte is added to the carrier total
levels on the way to $40 through $4E at every key on. A voice lifted out of a
game therefore carries no loudness at all, and playing one as it arrives puts a
single channel at the top of the part, which no driver does.

Measured over the corpus, taking the carrier total level standing in the shadow
registers at each key on and reading the carriers from the algorithm in $B0:

    files                  112
    key ons             120300

    quietest carrier       127
    upper quartile          30
    median                  22
    lower quartile          16
    loudest carrier          0
    mean                  24.2

    key ons at nought        18

A step is 0.75 dB, so the median sits 16.5 dB below the top of the part. Eighteen
key ons out of 120300 are at nought, which is the whole case for not resting a
voice there. `Patch.REST` is 22 and `Patch.rests` is what puts a preset on it.

The number is a driver convention rather than a property of the part. It is what
six channels summing into one lead leaves room for, and it is measured here
rather than taken from a document because no document states it.


## How much room samples have

The converter takes one byte at a time at `$2A` and one sample sounds at a time, so a figure in
bytes is never a count of what is sounding. It is storage, and where that storage is depends on
what the piece becomes:

| what it is exported as | what the samples cost |
| --- | --- |
| WAV, FLAC, Ogg Vorbis, Opus | nothing. They are mixed into the render and are not stored at all |
| VGM | nothing as a bank. Converter data is inline in the log, so a hit played twice is in the file twice |
| XGM | a real bank with real limits, below |
| a cartridge with the author's own driver | whatever the author set aside, which this application cannot know |

**XGM is the only one of those with a documented limit.** Its table holds 63 slots, each carrying an
offset and a length counted in units of 256 bytes; every sample is resampled to 14000 Hz and padded
up to that 256 byte boundary; and the driver mixes at most four of them at once in software.
`Xgm.SLOTS`, `Xgm.ALIGN`, `Xgm.PCM_RATE` and `Xgm.VOICES` are those four numbers, and
`Xgm.write` already counts what it had to drop past the first two.

The resample and the padding are not free, and they are why the figure in the document is not the
figure that reaches a driver:

| bank | in the document | in an XGM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the synthesised drum kit, 13 hits at 11025 Hz | 41393 | 54528, 1.32 times |
| the 808 kit, 10 hits at 11025 Hz | 80350 | 103168, 1.28 times |
| Sonic the Hedgehog, 4 hits | 40744 | 44288, 1.09 times |
| Sonic the Hedgehog 2, 8 hits | 49012 | 52736, 1.08 times |

The last two rows are what `mdd gate lift` recovered from recordings of those soundtracks at the
rates it measured. **That is what an extractor got out of a recording, not a reading of either
game's ROM**, and it is offered as a sense of what a game of the period spent rather than as a
limit of any kind.

**`Profile.sampleBytes` starts at 262144, and that is a convention with a reason rather than a
measurement.** The reason is a one megabyte cartridge with a quarter of it set aside for samples,
which is a size the machine shipped a great many of. It is written down as `Profile.ROM` so that
nobody has to guess what it meant, which is what went wrong the first time: the field was 65536
until it became 262144 inside a commit about key off timing, with nothing behind either number.

**No hardware fact is recoverable here, because there is not one.** The byte budget is a decision
about a cartridge, so it belongs to whoever is filling one, and the field is what an author sets
when they know their own. Nought means no limit, which is what the Master System uses, and
`Budget.overSong` warns above whatever it holds.

What the budget does instead is count what the music reaches for: each distinct sample some note
actually plays, once, at its own rate. A bank sitting in the library is not counted, because a
preset nobody triggers is in neither ROM nor RAM. The hardware panel shows that figure and draws
no bar where there is no limit to draw one against, which is the same thing it does for the
Master System.

## A PAL console

A PAL console's master clock is 53,203,424 Hz where an NTSC one's is 53,693,175, and the part takes
it divided by seven: 7,600,489 Hz rather than 7,670,453. Everything the part does counts in that
clock, so the same registers sound 0.99088 times as high, fifteen and a half cents lower, and every
envelope rate and every LFO rate slow by the same share. Rendered, a held A5 on the PAL clock comes
out 0.99095 times as high as on the NTSC one, against a ratio of 0.99088; the difference is the
zero crossing count over one second, not the part.

A driver that counts video frames is the larger difference. The same song data steps fifty times a
second rather than sixty, so a piece written for NTSC plays at five sixths of its tempo, which is
what a European release of a Japanese or American game is remembered for.
